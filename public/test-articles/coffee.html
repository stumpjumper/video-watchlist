The Long Road From Bean to Cup

According to legend, coffee was discovered in Ethiopia sometime in the ninth century by a goat herder named Kaldi, who noticed his animals became unusually energetic after eating berries from a certain tree. Whether or not Kaldi was real, coffee cultivation did originate in Ethiopia, and by the fifteenth century it had spread to the Arabian Peninsula, where Yemen became the first place to grow coffee commercially.

Coffee houses sprang up across the Arab world and became centers of social life — places where people gathered to talk, play chess, and exchange information. They were sometimes called Schools of the Wise. When coffee arrived in Europe in the seventeenth century, it quickly replaced beer and wine as the preferred morning drink, which had a noticeable effect on productivity across the continent.

Today more than two billion cups of coffee are consumed every day worldwide. The crop is grown in a belt spanning the tropics, from Brazil to Vietnam to Colombia, with each region producing beans that taste distinctly different from one another. Temperature, altitude, soil, and rainfall all shape the final flavor in ways that growers and roasters spend entire careers learning to read.
