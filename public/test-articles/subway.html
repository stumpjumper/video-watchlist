The First Subway Was a Secret

In 1870, a inventor named Alfred Ely Beach built a subway tunnel beneath Broadway in New York City and kept it hidden from the city's powerful political boss, William Tweed, who controlled most of what happened in Manhattan. Beach disguised his project as a mail-tube experiment, obtained a permit for a small pneumatic tube to carry packages, and then dug a full passenger tunnel underneath. He did it mostly at night to avoid detection.

The completed tunnel stretched one block and contained a single cylindrical car that was pushed back and forth by a giant fan. The waiting room at the Warren Street station was lavishly decorated, with a fountain, paintings on the walls, and a fish tank. When Beach opened it to the public, New Yorkers lined up for blocks to ride it. More than four hundred thousand people visited in the first year.

Tweed eventually shut it down through legal and political pressure, and the tunnel was sealed and forgotten. It was accidentally rediscovered in 1912 by workers digging the BMT Broadway Line, which still carries subway trains today. The old waiting room was found intact, including the car, still sitting on the tracks.
